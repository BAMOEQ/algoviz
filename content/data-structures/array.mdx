## What it is

An array is a single block of memory holding equally sized slots, one after another. That layout is
the whole story: because every slot is the same width and they sit next to each other, the address
of index `i` is just `base + i × width`. No searching, no pointer chasing — one multiplication.

**The invariant:** elements occupy a contiguous range of indices `0…n-1` with no gaps. Every
operation that inserts or removes has to preserve it, which is exactly why the middle is expensive.

## How operations work

### Access and update

`get(i)` and `set(i, v)` are arithmetic. They cost the same whether the array holds ten values or
ten million, and no other structure on this site beats them.

### Push and pop

Appending at the end is free when there is spare capacity. When there is not, the array allocates a
larger block — usually double — and copies everything across. That copy is `O(n)`, but it happens
rarely enough that the *amortized* cost of push stays `O(1)`.

### Insert and remove in the middle

Here the invariant bites. Inserting at index `i` means shifting every element from `i` onward one
slot right before writing; removing means shifting them back left. Both touch `n - i` elements, so
the closer to the front you work, the more it costs.

Step the demo through an `Insert at` and watch the write count: it is the number of shifted
elements plus one, every time.

The `O(1) amortized` on append is the entry worth understanding. Any individual push can be `O(n)`
if it triggers a resize, but averaged over many pushes the doubling strategy makes it constant.

## When to use it, and when not to

Reach for an array when you index more than you insert, when you iterate in order, or when you care
about cache behaviour — contiguous memory is the fastest thing a CPU can walk.

Reach for something else when you insert or delete at the front repeatedly (a linked list or a deque
does that in `O(1)`), when you need key lookup rather than positional lookup (a hash table), or when
you need ordered data with fast insertion (a BST or a heap).

The realistic comparison is against the linked list, and it is less flattering to lists than
textbooks suggest: lists win on `O(1)` splicing *given a node*, but finding that node is `O(n)` and
every step is a cache miss.

## Common pitfalls

- **Off-by-one on resize.** The new capacity must be at least `n + 1`, and the copy loop runs to
  the old length, not the new one.
- **Shifting in the wrong direction.** Inserting copies right-to-left; removing copies
  left-to-right. Reverse either and you smear one value across the array.
- **Assuming `insertAt(n)` is out of range.** Inserting *at* the end is valid — the legal range for
  insertion is `0…n`, one wider than for access.
- **Treating amortized as guaranteed.** In latency-sensitive code, a single resize copy can blow a
  frame budget even though the average is fine.
